The Rogue Valley's Go-To Source for Living Soil

Drive north on the Redwood Highway out of Grants Pass, hang a right past North Valley High School onto Monument Drive, and you'll find yourself in a different kind of supply yard. Daley Organics isn't a garden center with rows of packaged bags and seasonal displays. It's a working blending operation where Cameron Daley mixes premium organic soil, compost, and fertilizer in bulk — the kind of place where serious growers bring their trucks and load up by the yard.

Cameron started the operation in 2013 after growing up in the Rogue Valley. The idea was straightforward: build soil mixes that actually feed the biology underground — the mycorrhiza, the nematodes, the fungi networks — instead of just dumping synthetic nutrients on top. Twelve years later, that approach has earned a following that stretches well beyond Josephine County. Growers drive from Medford, Ashland, and as far as the Illinois Valley to pick up his blends.

The flagship premium potting soil isn't a simple peat-and-perlite mix. It packs over twenty organic amendments into each yard: worm castings, sea bird guano, bat guano, kelp meal, fish bone meal, feather meal, greensand, alfalfa meal, crab meal, shrimp meal, glacial rock dust, humic acids, and mycorrhizal inoculant, among others. That's roughly thirty pounds of organic fertilizer per cubic yard — roughly triple the amendment density of competing premium blends available at regional farm supply stores.

Beyond the premium mix, Daley Organics sells their Merlin blend (a lighter compost-based soil), cedar bark mulch, raw compost, pumice, perlite, peat moss, coconut coir, fill dirt, and standalone all-purpose and bloom fertilizers. Bulk pricing scales down with volume, and everything is available for pickup seven days a week.

The location sits on the edge of the Merlin community, about a mile past North Valley High School on the way toward the Hellgate Canyon stretch of the Rogue River. If you're rafting the Rogue or passing through on Highway 199 toward the Redwoods, it's a detour worth making — especially if you've been trying to figure out why your garden soil gives out after one season.
