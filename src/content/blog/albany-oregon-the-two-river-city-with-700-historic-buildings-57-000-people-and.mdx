## The Architecture Museum That Happens to Be a City

Albany, Oregon, has more historic buildings per capita than almost any city in the Pacific Northwest, and almost nobody outside the Willamette Valley knows about it.

Four National Register Historic Districts contain over 700 historic buildings spanning architectural styles from the 1860s to the 1920s. The Downtown Historic District alone features Italianate, Queen Anne, American Renaissance, Commercial Brick, Art Modern, and French Second Empire facades — the kind of architectural diversity that usually requires a European capital, not a farming town at the confluence of two Oregon rivers.

The Flinn Block at 222 First Avenue, built in 1887 by Judge L. Flinn, boasts one of the most ornate French Second Empire facades in the Northwest. The S. E. Young building, also 1887, was moved to its present location at 136 Lyon SW by teams of horses in 1914. These aren't reconstructions or theme-park replicas. They're the real thing — buildings that have been continuously used and maintained for over a century.

Albany's secret is that it was prosperous enough to build well in the 19th century and modest enough to avoid the urban renewal demolitions that destroyed historic downtowns across America in the 1960s and 1970s. What other cities tore down, Albany simply kept.

## The Two-River City

Albany sits at the confluence of the Calapooia River and the Willamette River, in both Linn and Benton counties. The city has been here since settlers founded it around 1848, and the river junction has been its defining geographic feature ever since — the reason the town exists, the reason it grew, and the reason the downtown developed where it did.

As the county seat of Linn County, Albany serves as the administrative and commercial center for one of Oregon's most productive agricultural regions. The Willamette Valley surrounding Albany produces grass seed, hay, mint, vegetables, nursery stock, and livestock. The city's economy mixes agriculture with manufacturing — a combination that has kept Albany economically stable even as other mid-valley towns have struggled.

## Growth Without Disruption

Albany's population is approximately 57,500, per World Population Review, making it the 11th most populous city in Oregon. The city grew by almost 39% between 2000 and 2020 — adding roughly 15,900 people — and is projected to reach nearly 72,000 by 2040 at its current growth rate of 1.27% annually.

The median household income is $78,114 with a poverty rate of 13.49%. The median age is 37.7 — solidly middle-aged, reflecting a city of working families and established households.

Albany's growth has been notable for what it hasn't disrupted. The historic districts remain intact. The downtown hasn't been hollowed out by suburban sprawl. The antique shops, galleries, and restaurants that fill the historic storefronts are real businesses serving real customers, not tourist-season operations that close in October.

## The Corvallis Connection

Albany and Corvallis — home of Oregon State University, 12 miles west — form a practical twin-city relationship. Many Albany residents work at OSU or in Corvallis, and many Corvallis workers live in Albany, where housing is more affordable. OR-34 connects the two cities via a quick 15-minute drive, making Albany a functional part of both the Salem and Corvallis economic orbits.

This dual gravity — Salem to the north, Corvallis to the west, Eugene to the south — gives Albany a stability that single-industry or single-anchor towns lack. The city draws employment, commerce, and cultural energy from three directions.

## Getting to Albany

**I-5 Exits 228, 233, and 234** provide access to Albany. Exit 233 leads most directly to the historic downtown area.

Albany is 25 miles south of Salem, 12 miles east of Corvallis, 45 miles north of Eugene, and approximately 70 miles south of Portland on I-5.
