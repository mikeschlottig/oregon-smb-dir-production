## The Town That Shouldn't Exist

Here is something that doesn't make sense on paper.

A town of 21,000 people — tucked against the Siskiyou Mountains at the California border, a solid five hours from Portland and four from San Francisco — draws roughly 400,000 visitors a year. Not for a national park. Not for a ski resort. For *theater*.

Ashland, Oregon, is the first city you encounter when you cross the state line heading north on Interstate 5. Exit 14 drops you into a place that, by every demographic and geographic measure, should be a quiet mountain rest stop. Instead, it's home to one of the oldest and largest professional repertory theater companies in the United States — the [Oregon Shakespeare Festival](https://www.osfashland.org/) — and an entire local economy that has been fundamentally reshaped by that single institution over the course of nine decades.

The question isn't whether Ashland is worth visiting. It obviously is. The question is *how* a town this small, this remote, built something this improbable — and what it tells you about the real economic engine underneath a place most people associate only with Shakespeare.

## The Festival That Built a City

In 1935, a young Southern Oregon Normal School professor named Angus L. Bowmer convinced the city to let him stage two Shakespeare plays inside the abandoned walls of a Chautauqua building. He paired them with boxing matches to hedge his bets on ticket sales. The boxing lost money. The Shakespeare didn't.

Ninety years later, the [Oregon Shakespeare Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Shakespeare_Festival) operates three theaters, runs a rotating repertory season from February through November, and has welcomed over 20 million visitors since its founding. In 2025, [OSF reported a 42% increase in ticket sales](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/01/22/oregon-shakespeare-festival-financially-successful-2025-season/) over the previous year, with nearly half of all theatergoers attending for the first time and the majority seeing three or more productions in a single visit.

That last detail matters. It's the difference between a tourist attraction and an economic engine. A visitor who stays one night and sees one show spends money at a hotel and a restaurant. A visitor who stays four nights and sees four shows spends money at four restaurants, two wine bars, a bookshop on East Main Street, and a morning coffee place they'll tell friends about when they get home.

According to the [Ashland Chamber of Commerce](https://www.ashlandchamber.com/), those visitors generate over $32 million annually in direct town revenue. The city's Transient Occupancy Tax — the lodging tax collected from hotels, B&Bs, and vacation rentals — brought in $1.1 million in a single quarter (July through September 2024), representing $11.1 million in visitor lodging spending in just three months. The same quarter set an all-time record for food and beverage tax collection at $867,580, according to [Travel Ashland's quarterly report](https://travelashland.com).

For a town with fewer people than most Portland neighborhoods, those are staggering numbers.

## Beyond the Stage: Lithia Park and the Siskiyous

The festival is the anchor, but Ashland's geography is the reason people come back.

[Lithia Park](https://ashlandoregon.gov) stretches 93 acres along Ashland Creek in the heart of downtown — designed in part by John McLaren, the same landscape architect behind San Francisco's Golden Gate Park. The park climbs from a Japanese garden and duck pond at the plaza entrance up through old-growth sycamores and conifers into the lower slopes of the Siskiyou Mountains. On a summer evening after a matinee at the outdoor Allen Elizabethan Theatre, the park fills with theatergoers, families, and Southern Oregon University students sprawled on the grass with books.

Five miles southeast of town, [Emigrant Lake](https://www.jacksoncountyor.gov/departments/parks/camping/emigrant_lake/) offers a different kind of afternoon. The 806-acre reservoir — formed by Emigrant Dam on Emigrant Creek as part of the federal Rogue River Project — is Jackson County Parks' most popular destination. It has a year-round RV park with 32 full-hookup sites, a 42-site campground, and a 280-foot twin-flume waterslide that is, improbably, one of the more photographed things in Southern Oregon. Fishing runs year-round for largemouth bass, smallmouth bass, crappie, and seasonally stocked rainbow trout.

For hikers, the real draw is the network of trails climbing into the Siskiyou Mountains south and east of town. The Pacific Crest Trail passes within miles of Ashland, and the [Siskiyou Summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siskiyou_Summit) — at 4,310 feet, the highest point on Interstate 5 between Mexico and Canada — marks the dramatic geographic threshold between Oregon and California. Driving north over the summit and descending into the Rogue Valley toward Ashland is one of the more cinematic arrivals on any interstate in the West.

## The Food and Wine Scene That Punches Up

Ashland supports nearly 100 restaurants for a population of 21,000 — a ratio that would be remarkable in a city ten times its size. The density exists because of the visitor economy, but the quality exists because of the culture the festival created.

The Rogue Valley's climate — warm, dry summers with cool nights in the 1,500–2,500 foot elevation band — turns out to be excellent for growing Tempranillo, Syrah, and Viognier. The region's wineries have gained national recognition, and Ashland sits at the southern end of the [Rogue Valley AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_Valley_AVA) wine trail that extends north through the Bear Creek Valley toward Medford and Central Point. Tasting rooms are scattered through downtown, several within walking distance of the festival theaters.

The restaurant scene leans independent and farm-to-table. This is not a town of chains. The proximity to Rogue Valley farms, the demands of a well-traveled and food-aware visitor base, and the influence of a university town's progressive culture have produced a dining scene that's more Bend or Healdsburg than rural Southern Oregon.

## The Economy Behind the Curtain

Beneath the tourism numbers, Ashland's economy is more complex — and more fragile — than it appears.

[Southern Oregon University](https://sou.edu/) is the city's largest employer, with over 750 faculty and administrative staff and approximately 6,000 students. SOU contributes a year-round population base that keeps businesses alive outside the peak theater season, and its programs in environmental science, business, and the arts feed directly into the local workforce.

The [Ashland Chamber of Commerce](https://www.ashlandchamber.com/page.asp?navid=1234) reports that the city is home to over 1,200 firms employing approximately 10,830 workers, with total payroll exceeding $400 million. The self-employment rate in Ashland is 16.7% — more than double the statewide average of 7.4% — driven in part by the [Ashland Fiber Network (AFN)](https://ashlandoregon.gov), a municipally-owned broadband system that gives the town connectivity infrastructure unusual for a city its size. That fiber network helped Ashland become an early "Zoom town" during the pandemic, attracting remote workers drawn to the quality of life and small-town scale.

But there are real pressures. Lodging tax revenue — a critical line item in the city's general fund — has not fully recovered to pre-2019 levels when adjusted for inflation, according to [Ashland Forward](https://www.ashlandforward.com/issues/city-government/economy). Wildfire smoke from increasingly severe fire seasons has disrupted peak summer tourism. Climate events and changing visitor patterns have introduced volatility into what was once a predictable seasonal cycle.

## Housing: Beautiful, Expensive, and Complicated

The same qualities that make Ashland a desirable place to visit make it an expensive place to live.

As of early 2026, the average home value in Ashland is approximately $550,600, according to [Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/home-values/30275/ashland-or/) — down about 1% year-over-year but still well above the Jackson County median. Homes go to pending in roughly 26 days. Inventory in the Ashland market was up 17.1% compared to the prior year as of mid-2025, according to the [Ashland Chronicle](https://ashland.news/real-estate-corner-navigating-the-2025-housing-market/), giving buyers somewhat more leverage than in the tighter pandemic-era market.

The housing challenge is structural. Ashland is physically constrained by its geography — mountains to the south and east, an urban growth boundary, and limited buildable land. The workforce that keeps the restaurants, hotels, and festival infrastructure running often can't afford to live in the city they serve. Many commute from Talent, Phoenix, or Medford, where housing costs are significantly lower.

This tension — between being a place people want to live and being a place working people can afford to live — is one of the defining dynamics of Ashland's next chapter.

## Getting to Ashland

Ashland is the first Oregon city on I-5 northbound from California. **Exit 14** provides access to south Ashland and the Siskiyou Boulevard corridor. **Exit 19** (OR-66) connects to east Ashland, the route to Emigrant Lake, and the Greensprings Highway toward Klamath Falls.

[Rogue Valley International-Medford Airport (MFR)](https://www.jacksoncountyor.gov/airport) is 15 miles north and serves direct flights from several western cities. Ashland is approximately 285 miles south of Portland (4.5 hours), 350 miles north of San Francisco (5.5 hours), and 70 miles north of the California state line.

Amtrak's Coast Starlight route stops in nearby Klamath Falls (approximately 65 miles east via OR-66), though most visitors arrive by car.
