## The Utopian Colony That Became an Antiques Capital

In 1856, a Prussian-born preacher named William Keil led nearly 600 followers across the Oregon Trail to build a utopian society in the Willamette Valley. He named the settlement after his daughter: Aurora.

The Aurora Colony was a Christian communal society — one of America's longest-surviving utopian experiments. At its peak in 1868, the colony held about 600 people and 15,000 acres of land. Members shared property, labor, and meals. They built an Ox Barn (1862), a Kraus House (1874), a Steinbach Cabin (1876), and a Wash House (1877) — structures that still stand today. The colony was formally dissolved in 1883, but its buildings and its ethos endured.

In 1974, approximately 150 acres and 12 buildings of the former colony were inscribed on the National Register of Historic Places as the **Aurora Colony Historic District** — Oregon's first National Historic District. The city later adopted a Historic Overlay District in 1988, ensuring that all exterior work on buildings preserves the community's visual character.

Today, Aurora is Oregon's antiques capital. [Twenty-three shops](https://traveloregon.com/things-to-do/culture-history/historic-sites-oregon-trail/aurora-colony-national-historic-district/), many in historic buildings, line Main Street with some of America's best antiques and vintage stores, galleries, eateries, and a candy factory. Aurora Mills Architectural Salvage offers reclaimed architectural pieces in the heart of town. The [Aurora Colony Museum](https://www.auroracolony.org/) provides guided tours and changing exhibits exploring the colony's history and legacy.

Nearby, the **Gordon House** — the only Frank Lloyd Wright-designed building in Oregon — adds another layer of architectural significance.

## Small, Affluent, and Deliberately Preserved

Aurora's population is approximately 1,112, per World Population Review, declining slightly at -0.45% annually. The **median household income is $107,500** with a poverty rate of 6.15%. The **median age is 52** — the oldest of any town on the I-5 corridor — reflecting established homeowners, retirees, and the kind of residents who choose a historic village over suburban convenience.

The city is less than half a square mile, deliberately compact and carefully preserved. The historic district zoning means Aurora doesn't just look historic — it's legally required to maintain its character. This is a community that has chosen preservation over growth, and the economics support the choice.

## Getting to Aurora

**I-5 Exit 278** (Aurora/Donald) provides access to Aurora. The town is approximately 1 mile west of the interchange along Ehlen Road and Main Street.

Aurora is 25 miles south of Portland, 7 miles north of Woodburn, and 35 miles north of Salem on I-5.
