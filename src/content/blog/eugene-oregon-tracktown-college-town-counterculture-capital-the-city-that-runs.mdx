## The City That Runs

Eugene, Oregon, is the city that literally invented American jogging.

In the 1960s, University of Oregon track coach Bill Bowerman returned from a trip to New Zealand, where he'd observed the local fitness craze of recreational running. He brought the idea back to Eugene and enlisted his Duck athletes as personal instructors for the community. Thousands of locals started running. Bowerman published a book called *Jogging* in 1967. The rest of America eventually followed.

Bowerman also co-founded Nike with his former runner Phil Knight, pouring rubber into a waffle iron in his Eugene garage to create the first Nike sole. The company's spiritual home is Eugene — Knight's hometown, Bowerman's proving ground, and the place where the culture of competitive running became inseparable from daily life.

Hayward Field is the cathedral. Originally built in 1919, the stadium was rebuilt in 2020 into a 12,650-seat, world-class track-only facility that hosted the 2022 World Athletics Championships — the first time the event was held in the United States. The 2021 U.S. Olympic Trials were held here. The 2026 World Athletics U20 Championships are coming next. TrackTown USA manages the events calendar, and the annual Prefontaine Classic brings the world's fastest athletes to a college town of 179,000 people.

Steve Prefontaine, the legendary Oregon distance runner who died in a car accident in 1975 at age 24, is memorialized throughout the city — at Pre's Trail along the Willamette, at the site of the crash on Skyline Boulevard, and in the ethos of a community that still believes running is a form of civic identity.

## The University and the City

The University of Oregon is not just *in* Eugene — it *is* a significant part of Eugene. With over 24,000 students, the university shapes the city's economy, culture, demographics, and politics. The campus, designed in the 1910s with an emphasis on native landscaping, is one of the most beautiful in the Pacific Northwest, anchored by century-old trees, the Pioneer Mother statue, and the Knight Library.

The Ducks are a major cultural force. Oregon football, playing in the 54,000-seat Autzen Stadium, draws national attention, and the university's athletic department — heavily backed by Nike co-founder Phil Knight — has transformed the school's brand into one of the most visible in college sports. But the real heartbeat is track and field. Always has been.

Beyond athletics, the university's schools of journalism, music, architecture, and environmental studies have shaped Eugene's identity as a city of ideas, creativity, and progressive activism. The campus area along 13th Avenue and Agate Street is dense with bookstores, coffee shops, and the kind of conversations you'd expect in a college town that takes itself seriously without entirely losing its sense of humor.

## Counterculture Capital

Eugene's counterculture credentials are deep and genuine.

The Oregon Country Fair, held annually each July in nearby Veneta, draws tens of thousands of visitors for a three-day festival of art, music, food, and intentional weirdness that has been running since 1969. The Saturday Market — the oldest continuously operating open-air crafts market in the United States, founded in 1970 — fills downtown's Park Blocks every Saturday from April through November with local artisans, food vendors, and musicians.

Ken Kesey, author of *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*, called Eugene's surrounding area home. The Merry Pranksters' legacy is woven into the city's fabric, alongside a long tradition of environmental activism, cooperative housing, and the kind of earnest community organizing that makes Eugene simultaneously admirable and satirizable.

The city's bumper stickers tell the story: "Keep Eugene Weird" is not ironic here. It's municipal policy.

## The Food and Drink Scene

Eugene's food scene punches well above its weight for a city of 179,000.

The Willamette Valley's proximity to some of the richest agricultural land in the Pacific Northwest means farm-to-table dining is not a marketing concept — it's a supply chain. Local restaurants draw on the valley's grass-fed beef, Willamette Valley wines (particularly Pinot Noir from the surrounding AVAs), Oregon hazelnuts, seasonal produce from the Lane County Farmers' Market, and some of the finest craft beer in a state that takes craft beer very seriously.

Ninkasi Brewing, Oakshire Brewing, and Claim 52 Brewing are Eugene originals. The Whiteaker neighborhood ("the Whit") has become a hub for breweries, cideries, and small-batch producers. Fifth Street Public Market, a converted industrial space, houses restaurants, specialty shops, and a food hall that captures the city's aesthetic: locally sourced, casually ambitious, and unapologetically Oregon.

## The Numbers

Eugene's population is approximately 179,000, making it the third-largest city in Oregon behind Portland and Salem. The median household income is $66,562, per World Population Review, with a poverty rate of 19.04% — higher than the national average, partly reflecting the large student population. The median age is 35.2, younger than most Oregon cities of comparable size.

The city has been growing slowly (0.05% annually), with deaths outpacing births in Lane County — a trend offset by continued inbound migration. Oregon's population growth is increasingly driven by people moving in rather than being born here, and Eugene's combination of quality of life, outdoor access, and cultural richness continues to attract newcomers.

## Getting Around

Eugene has one of the better public transit systems for an Oregon city its size. Lane Transit District (LTD) operates the EmX bus rapid transit line connecting Eugene, Springfield, and surrounding communities. The city's extensive bike path network — including the Ruth Bascom Riverbank Path system along the Willamette — makes cycling a genuine transportation option, not just recreation.

## Getting to Eugene

**I-5 Exits 192, 194, 195, and 199** provide access to Eugene. The city straddles the Willamette River, with downtown Eugene on the west bank and the University of Oregon campus to the south.

Eugene is 110 miles south of Portland, 65 miles south of Salem, and 70 miles north of Roseburg on I-5. The Eugene Airport (EUG) offers direct flights to major West Coast hubs.
