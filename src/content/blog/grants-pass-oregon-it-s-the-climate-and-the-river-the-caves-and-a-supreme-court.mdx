## The Sign and What's Behind It

If you've driven Interstate 5 through Southern Oregon, you've seen the sign.

A neon-bright arch over the road at the southern entrance to Grants Pass reads: **"It's the Climate."** The city erected it in 1920. A century later, it still works. Grants Pass gets 55 inches less annual rainfall than Astoria on the Oregon coast and enjoys a Mediterranean-style climate — warm, dry summers and mild winters — that feels more like Northern California than the Pacific Northwest stereotype of perpetual gray.

The sign was designed to attract tourists. It succeeded far beyond anyone's 1920 intentions. Grants Pass is now a city of roughly 39,500 people — the county seat of Josephine County, the 15th most populous city in Oregon, and the self-proclaimed gateway to the Wild and Scenic Rogue River. It sits on I-5 halfway between San Francisco and Portland, which means every traveler driving the West Coast corridor passes through.

But in June 2024, Grants Pass became nationally known for something that had nothing to do with climate or rivers. The [United States Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-175_19m2.pdf) ruled 6-3 in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* that cities may enforce anti-camping ordinances against people experiencing homelessness — even when no shelter beds are available. The landmark decision overturned years of Ninth Circuit precedent and returned the question of how to address unsheltered homelessness entirely to local governments.

Grants Pass didn't ask to become a constitutional test case. But the case — and the national attention it brought — revealed something true about the city: it is a place where real beauty and real tension coexist, where the climate draws people in and the cost of living pushes others to the margins. Understanding Grants Pass means holding both realities at once.

## Hellgate Canyon and the Wild Rogue

The Rogue River runs through Grants Pass, and the river is the reason most visitors come.

Downstream from town, the Rogue enters [Hellgate Canyon](https://hellgate.com/) — a narrow, dramatic gorge where basalt walls rise hundreds of feet above the water. [Hellgate Jetboat Excursions](https://hellgate.com/) has been running guided trips through the canyon since the 1950s, offering everything from scenic brunch cruises to whitewater blasts that push through rapids and spray passengers in the process. The company operates its own Hellgate River Lodge, accessible only by boat, where guests dine overlooking the river.

Beyond Hellgate, the Rogue continues into its federally designated Wild and Scenic corridor — one of the original eight rivers protected under the 1968 National Wild and Scenic Rivers Act. The multi-day rafting trips through the Rogue River canyon are among the most coveted wilderness experiences in the western United States, with permit lotteries that fill months in advance. Outfitters like [Morrison's Rogue Wilderness Adventures](https://rogueriverraft.com/) and [Noah's Wilderness Adventures](https://noahsroguerivertrips.com/) launch from access points near Grants Pass and the nearby community of Merlin, offering 3- to 4-day camping and lodge trips through Class I-IV whitewater.

Closer to town, the Rogue is gentler — wide, warm, and popular for swimming, tubing, and casual paddling through the summer months. Riverside Park and Schroeder Park provide public access points within city limits. The contrast between the mellow in-town river and the wild canyon downstream is part of what makes Grants Pass's relationship with the Rogue so distinctive.

## Oregon Caves and the Siskiyou Country

Grants Pass is the primary gateway to one of Oregon's most unusual natural attractions.

[Oregon Caves National Monument and Preserve](https://www.nps.gov/orca/) is located 50 miles southwest of Grants Pass, deep in the Siskiyou Mountains near Cave Junction. The 4,554-acre park, managed by the National Park Service, protects a marble cave system carved by millennia of acidic water seeping through rock. Guided tours take visitors through the "Marble Halls of Oregon" — twisting passages lined with stalactites, stalagmites, flowstone, and formations that look like they belong on another planet.

The drive from Grants Pass to the caves via US-199 (the Redwood Highway) is an attraction in itself — descending through the Illinois Valley into increasingly wild, forested country. Cave Junction, the small town at the turnoff to the monument, serves as the last supply point before the winding climb to the caves.

For visitors based in Grants Pass, the caves make an ideal day trip — and the Redwood Highway continues south into California's Jedediah Smith Redwoods State Park, making Grants Pass a genuine crossroads between Oregon's Rogue Valley and the Northern California coast.

## Food, Drink, and Fire Mountain Gems

Grants Pass's downtown has more character than its freeway exits suggest.

The [Grants Pass Growers Market](https://visitgrantspass.com/) — a Saturday farmers market running from March through November — is one of the largest in Southern Oregon, featuring local produce, artisan food, baked goods, and crafts. The surrounding downtown blocks support independent restaurants, brewpubs, coffee shops, and tasting rooms that reflect the town's outdoors-oriented culture.

Two businesses give Grants Pass an economic identity beyond tourism. [Fire Mountain Gems and Beads](https://www.firemountaingems.com/) — headquartered at 1 Fire Mountain Way — is one of the world's largest jewelry-making supply companies, employing roughly 450 people and shipping globally from Grants Pass. It's the kind of company you wouldn't expect to find in a river town of 39,000.

And while Dutch Bros Coffee is now a national chain traded on the NYSE, it was founded in Grants Pass in 1992 by brothers Dane and Travis Boersma, who started with a single espresso pushcart. The company's cultural DNA — friendly, fast, community-oriented — was shaped by this town before it scaled to over 900 locations.

[Wildlife Images Rehabilitation and Education Center](https://visitgrantspass.com/), located south of town, is a nonprofit wildlife rehabilitation facility where visitors can see bears, cougars, birds of prey, and other native animals in a sanctuary setting. It's one of the region's most-visited family attractions.

## The Economy Behind the River

Grants Pass is the economic center of Josephine County — a county with a complicated fiscal history and a workforce shaped by the same timber collapse that reshaped all of Southern Oregon.

The city's economy employs approximately 17,100 people, according to [Data USA](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/grants-pass-or). The largest industries are healthcare and social assistance (3,196 jobs), retail trade (2,617), and manufacturing (1,689). The top employers in Josephine County include [Rogue Community College](https://www.roguecc.edu/) (919 employees), Three Rivers Hospital (900), Grants Pass School District (670), Three Rivers School District (645), Fire Mountain Gems (450), and Josephine County government (450).

The visitor industry contributed 2,230 jobs across Josephine County in 2022, according to [Visit Grants Pass's annual report](https://visitgrantspass.com/). Tourism is seasonal and river-dependent, peaking in summer when rafting, jetboating, and fishing draw visitors from across the West.

Grants Pass is also, increasingly, a destination for remote workers and retirees drawn by the climate, the river access, and housing costs lower than Portland, Bend, or the Bay Area. The tension this creates — between longtime residents and newcomers, between outdoor-recreation identity and growth pressure — is the undercurrent beneath the city's cheerful "It's the Climate" brand.

## Housing: Affordable Compared to Where You're Coming From

Grants Pass's housing market depends entirely on your frame of reference.

The average home value is approximately $398,231 as of early 2026, according to [Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/home-values/31829/grants-pass-or/) — down slightly (0.3%) year-over-year. The median sale price tracked by [Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/city/7804/OR/Grants-Pass/housing-market) sits around $360,000, with homes spending an average of 53 days on market. Some sources show higher medians depending on the month — the market is variable, with individual transactions ranging widely from starter homes in the low $300,000s to riverfront properties well above $500,000.

Compared to Portland ($525,000+), Bend ($650,000+), or anywhere in the Bay Area, Grants Pass looks like a bargain. Compared to the local economy — where the median family household income is [$49,335](https://www.grantspassoregon.gov/169/Population-Economic-Statistics) according to the city's own data — a $400,000 home represents a significant financial stretch.

This gap between what the market costs and what local wages support is the same tension that drove the homelessness crisis underlying the Supreme Court case. Grants Pass is affordable by Portland standards, expensive by local ones, and a place where the distance between those two frames defines the housing conversation.

## Getting to Grants Pass

**I-5 Exit 55** (US-199 / Redwood Highway) provides access to southern Grants Pass and the route southwest to Cave Junction, Oregon Caves, and the Northern California coast. **Exit 58** (6th Street) connects to downtown Grants Pass and the Rogue River.

Grants Pass is approximately 30 miles northwest of Medford, 250 miles south of Portland (4 hours), and 340 miles north of San Francisco (5.5 hours). [Rogue Valley International-Medford Airport (MFR)](https://www.jacksoncountyor.gov/airport) is 30 miles southeast. The city is also accessible via US-199 from Crescent City, California, and the southern Oregon coast.
