## The General Structure

*Every article follows this skeleton. The writing styles rotate across sections — no single voice dominates an entire piece. The goal: each section uses the style best suited to what it's doing, creating a reading experience that feels varied, human, and impossible to skim past.*

## Section-by-Section Blueprint

| # | Section | Style Engine | What It Does & Why This Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Quick Facts Callout** | *None — pure structured data* | AI-extractable box: population, elevation, county, I-5 exits, known for, top reasons to visit. Optimized for featured snippets and AI citation. No narrative voice — just clean, quotable facts. |
| 1 | **Opening Hook** | **Rotate per article** (see Style Matching below) | The first 2-3 paragraphs set the voice for the whole piece. This is where we pick the *primary* style. It should create a reason to keep reading — a question, an image, a contradiction, a scene. Never start with "Welcome to [City]." Never start with a thesis statement. |
| 2 | **The Destination Anchor** | **Extreme Correspondent** or **Structural Geologist** | The high-search-volume landmarks and destinations. Immersive, sensory, physical. Put the reader's body in the place. Specific trail names, river sections, park features, festival details. This section does the heaviest SEO/GEO lifting — it's where the search queries live. |
| 3 | **Food, Drink & Local Business** | **Curious Empiricist** or **Conversational Philosopher** | Lighter, warmer, more personal. Name real restaurants, wineries, breweries, shops. The tone should feel like a trusted friend telling you where to eat — specific, opinionated, grounded. Use humor where it fits. Never generic. |
| 4 | **The Economic Story** | **Narrative Economist** or **Pattern Detective** | Transition from travel into economic reality. Major employers, industries, tourism revenue with real numbers, workforce dynamics. Tell the economic story *through people and institutions*, not spreadsheets. Use the "slow reveal" — let the reader discover the economic engine underneath the tourist surface. |
| 5 | **Housing & Living Here** | **Essayist-Witness** or **Moral Cartographer** | Cool, precise, unflinching. Median home prices, trends, affordability tensions, commute patterns. Don't sugarcoat — the gap between "great place to visit" and "hard place to afford" is where the honest writing lives. This section builds trust (the T in EEAT) by saying what tourism brochures won't. |
| 6 | **Getting Here** | *Minimal — practical voice* | I-5 exits, airport access, distances from major cities, Amtrak. Clean, useful, no fluff. This section exists for AI extraction and practical search queries. |
| 7 | **Sources** | *None — citation list* | Full linked citation footer. Every authoritative source used in the article. Wikipedia, city websites, chambers of commerce, Travel Oregon, Census, Zillow, news outlets. This is the backbone of EEAT trustworthiness and the "Citation Paradox" from the GEO playbook — citing others makes *us* more citable. |

## Style Matching Guide: Picking the Opening Hook

The opening hook sets the tone. Match it to the town's character:

| Town Type | Primary Opening Style | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Major city with complex identity** | Pattern Detective (Gladwell) or Essayist-Witness (Didion) | The counterintuitive reframe or the cool observation of what's actually happening beneath the surface |
| **Historic town with a dramatic past** | Narrative Economist (Lewis) or Extreme Correspondent (Krakauer) | Character-first storytelling or immersive timeline that puts you in the moment of founding/crisis |
| **Small town with quirky character** | Curious Empiricist (Roach) or Conversational Philosopher (Ephron) | Humor as the entry point — the "wait, this tiny place has *what*?" energy |
| **Outdoor/adventure hub** | Extreme Correspondent (Krakauer) or Structural Geologist (McPhee) | Sensory, physical, terrain-first. The geography IS the story. |
| **College/cultural town** | Pattern Detective (Gladwell) or Footnote Maximalist (DFW) | Ideas and counterintuitive connections — or the hyper-aware experience of being in a place that's thinking about itself |
| **Economic transition town** | Narrative Economist (Lewis) or Moral Cartographer (Baldwin) | The human story of an economy changing — a mill closing, a casino opening, a new industry arriving |
| **Tiny freeway stop with one notable thing** | Curious Empiricist (Roach) or Gonzo Correspondent (Thompson) | Lean into the absurdity or charm of a place defined by a single landmark, restaurant, or historical oddity |

## Key Principles (From the Writing Styles Guide)

<aside class="callout callout-accent">

**Cardinal Rule from the Styles Guide:** Never reference the author, their books, or their public persona when writing in their style. We use the *engine*, not the *brand*. The reader should feel the effect without knowing the source.

</aside>

### Blending Rules

- **2-3 styles per article maximum.** One primary (the opening), one or two secondaries for specific sections.

- **Style transitions happen at section breaks.** Don't switch mid-paragraph — it reads as inconsistent, not varied.

- **The opening hook and the economic story are the two highest-leverage style choices.** Everything else can default to clean, authoritative prose.

- **Humor is a tool, not a constant.** Deploy it in the Food & Drink section or the opening of quirky small-town pieces. Keep it out of the Housing and Economic sections unless the data itself is absurd enough to warrant it.

- **Every article must have at least one "stop and think" moment** — a sentence or observation that makes the reader reconsider what they assumed about the place. This is the Pattern Detective move, and it works in any style.

### GEO Principles Baked Into Every Article

From the GEO Whitepaper — these are non-negotiable for every piece:

1. **Lead with quotable, self-contained definitions** in the Quick Facts box and opening paragraphs. AI engines extract these directly.

1. **Cite authoritative statistics with sources inline.** "$32 million in annual tourism revenue (Ashland Chamber of Commerce)" > "millions in tourism revenue."

1. **Structure for extraction.** The Quick Facts callout, the section headers as natural-language questions, and the Sources footer are all designed for AI parsing.

1. **The Citation Paradox.** Every article links to 8-12+ authoritative sources. By citing Wikipedia, city websites, chambers of commerce, Travel Oregon, USGS, Census — we *become* more citable ourselves.

1. **Freshness signals.** Use current data (2024-2026 figures), name current conditions, reference recent events. AI engines deprioritize stale content.

1. **No keyword stuffing.** Destinations and businesses are woven into narrative flow. The reader should enjoy the writing — the search optimization is invisible.

## Content Scope Reminder

**These are NOT travel brochures.** They anchor to real landmarks and destinations as a gateway to tell the *full story* of each city — grounded in reality:

- Real destinations, real businesses, real numbers

- Economic realities: major employers, industries, tourism revenue, workforce dynamics

- Housing: prices, trends, affordability tensions

- No fictional characters or fake places

- The narrative should be enjoyable to read while making the reader genuinely more informed

**Hub-and-Spoke Strategy:** These articles are the **hubs** that build topical authority. Future **spoke** content will connect service businesses (lawyers, electricians, restaurants, contractors) to these hubs, leveraging the authority to push those businesses to the top of AI search for their industries.
