## The City That Isn't Salem (But You'd Be Forgiven for Thinking So)

Keizer has an identity problem, and it knows it.

With approximately 39,000 people, Keizer is the 16th largest city in Oregon — larger than Grants Pass, Ashland, or Cottage Grove, and within striking distance of Oregon City and Redmond on the population charts. But ask most Oregonians where Keizer is, and they'll say "near Salem" or "north of Salem" or just "Salem."

This is geographically accurate and culturally incomplete. Keizer is not a Salem neighborhood. It's an independently incorporated city with its own government, its own schools (Salem-Keizer School District, shared), its own commercial centers, and its own sensibility — slightly more suburban, slightly more family-oriented, and significantly more invested in minor league baseball than its larger neighbor.

## Oregon's Field of Dreams

Roto-Rooter Park (renamed in 2025 after a naming rights deal with the plumbing company, because American minor league baseball is nothing if not commercially creative) sits adjacent to Interstate 5, just beyond the right field fence. Originally opened as Volcanoes Stadium in 1997, the 4,254-seat park is home to the Salem-Keizer Volcanoes and, more recently, the Mavericks Independent Baseball League.

The park earned an honorable mention from the Salem chapter of the American Institute of Architects in 1999, and it's earned something harder to quantify from the community: genuine affection. The Volcanoes won five Northwest League championships (1998, 2001, 2006, 2007, 2009), and the park's location — visible from the interstate, set in a complex that includes training facilities and youth programs — makes it a community anchor in a way that a strip mall never could.

Minor league baseball parks are civic investments that often define small cities more effectively than government buildings or corporate campuses. Roto-Rooter Park is Keizer's signature.

## Suburban by Design

Keizer's development pattern is suburban — residential neighborhoods, commercial corridors, chain retail, and the Keizer Station shopping complex near I-5 that serves as the city's commercial center. McNary Estates, at the north end of the city, provides easy access to I-5, River Road, and Keizer Station.

The median household income is $84,813, per World Population Review, with a poverty rate of 8.05% — both better than Salem's figures, reflecting Keizer's role as the slightly more affluent suburban complement to the capital city. The median age is 37.4, and the population is stable to slightly declining (-0.11% annually).

Keizer's Willamette River frontage along its western edge provides natural amenity access. Keizer Rapids Park is the crown jewel — a large riverside park with trails, sports fields, playgrounds, and river access that gives the city a genuine outdoor identity beyond its commercial corridors.

## The Salem-Keizer Relationship

Keizer and Salem share a school district (Salem-Keizer), a housing market, a commute pattern, and a metropolitan identity. But they don't share a government, and Keizer residents will point this out with varying degrees of intensity depending on how recently someone has called their city "north Salem."

The relationship is functional and occasionally competitive — two cities that are better together economically but distinct enough culturally to justify separate incorporation. Keizer is more suburban, more family-focused, and more likely to define community around parks and baseball than government and universities.

## Getting to Keizer

**I-5 Exit 260** provides access to Keizer. The city is directly north of Salem along I-5 and River Road.

Keizer is directly adjacent to Salem, 45 miles south of Portland, and approximately 260 miles north of the California border on I-5.
