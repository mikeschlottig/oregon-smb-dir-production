## The Company You've Never Heard Of

Somewhere in a nondescript office building on North Bartlett Street in Medford, Oregon — a city most Americans couldn't locate on a map if you gave them the state outline and a red pen — sits the headquarters of the 124th largest company in the United States.

Lithia Motors. Now operating as [Lithia & Driveway](https://www.lithia.com/). NYSE ticker: LAD. Revenue in 2024: [$36.2 billion](https://investors.lithiadriveway.com/press-releases/mbgcm087x1aqfy93pgf). Thirty thousand employees. The largest automotive retailer on Earth.

The company started in 1946 when a man named Walt DeBoer opened a single car dealership in Ashland — 13 miles south — and sold 14 vehicles in his first year. His son Sid moved the operation to Medford in 1970. Today, Lithia operates hundreds of dealerships across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada, and it [rose to #124 on the 2025 Fortune 500](https://investors.lithiadriveway.com/press-releases/mbgcm087x1aqfy93pgf), climbing 16 spots from the previous year.

This is the first thing to understand about Medford: it is not what it looks like from the interstate.

From I-5, Medford presents as a standard-issue Western American city — big-box retail corridors, hospital campuses, chain hotels stacked along the exits. Travelers blow through at 65 miles per hour on their way to Ashland or Crater Lake. But underneath that unremarkable interstate surface is a city with a $36 billion corporate headquarters, a 115-year-old luxury food brand that invented the mail-order gift basket, a creamery that produces the best blue cheese on the planet, and two seven-million-year-old volcanic plateaus rising 800 feet above the valley floor five minutes from downtown.

Medford is the hub that makes all of Southern Oregon work. The question worth asking is *how* a city this far from any major metro built an economy this layered — and what it actually looks like when you stop.

## Seven Million Years of Table Rock

The most spectacular thing within Medford's orbit requires a short drive north and a moderate hike.

[Upper and Lower Table Rock](https://www.blm.gov/programs/recreation/recreation-activities/oregon-washington/tablerocks) are twin volcanic plateaus rising roughly 800 feet above the Rogue Valley floor, just northeast of the city near Central Point. They were formed approximately seven million years ago when a basalt lava flow from the upper Rogue River filled an ancient river channel. The surrounding softer rock eroded away over millennia. The lava cap didn't. What remains are two flat-topped mesas standing at about 2,000 feet above sea level — geological artifacts that look, from certain angles, like someone sliced the tops off mountains with a blade.

The [Bureau of Land Management](https://www.blm.gov/programs/recreation/recreation-activities/oregon-washington/tablerocks/plan-your-visit) and The Nature Conservancy jointly manage 4,871 acres across both formations. Upper Table Rock is the shorter hike — roughly 2.5 miles round trip with 720 feet of elevation gain — and rewards with panoramic views across the entire Rogue Valley. Lower Table Rock is longer and steeper at 6.3 miles round trip. Both summits host vernal pools: seasonal wetlands that fill with winter rain and support species found nowhere else, including the threatened dwarf woolly meadowfoam.

The Table Rocks were a sanctuary for the Takelma people long before European settlement. Their cultural and ecological significance makes them one of Southern Oregon's most important natural landmarks — and one of its most popular hikes. Spring wildflower season, typically March through May, transforms the mesa tops into carpets of color that draw hikers from across the region.

Below the Table Rocks, the [Bear Creek Greenway](https://jacksoncountyor.gov/departments/parks/greenway/bear_creek_greenway.php) offers a flatter alternative — a paved multi-use trail running roughly 20 miles through the valley from Ashland to Central Point, passing through Medford along Bear Creek. It connects directly to city parks, neighborhoods, and downtown, and serves as the recreational spine of the Rogue Valley.

## Pears, Cheese, and the Rogue Valley Palate

Medford's food story starts with a pear.

In 1910, a Seattle hotelier named Samuel Rosenberg traded his hotel for 240 acres of pear orchards along Bear Creek. His sons — Harry and David Holmes, both Cornell agricultural graduates — inherited the operation and began marketing [Comice pears](https://www.harryanddavid.com/articles/from-the-orchards/royal-riviera-pear-history) as a luxury product to East Coast buyers. The Rogue Valley's climate turned out to produce Comice pears superior to the French originals. The brothers branded them Royal Riviera®, and in 1934 launched what became the modern gift-basket industry: premium fruit, packed and shipped directly to the customer.

[Harry & David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_%26_David) is still headquartered in Medford. The company has changed ownership multiple times — including bankruptcy and acquisition — but the orchards along Bear Creek remain, and the Harry & David Country Village store on the south side of town is a destination for visitors and locals alike. The [Oregon Encyclopedia](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/harry_david/) credits the Rosenberg family's operation with helping establish Southern Oregon's identity as premium agricultural country.

Twelve miles north in Central Point, [Rogue Creamery](https://roguecreamery.com/pages/our-story) makes the case that the Rogue Valley's food reputation extends well beyond pears. Founded in 1928 as a dairy cooperative, the creamery was purchased in 2002 by David Gremmels and Cary Bryant, who transformed it into one of America's most decorated artisan cheesemakers. Their [Rogue River Blue](https://roguecreamery.com/products/rogue-river-blue-cheese) — a seasonal cheese made from fall milk, aged 9 to 11 months, and hand-wrapped in organic Syrah grape leaves soaked in pear spirits — won [World Champion at the 2019 World Cheese Awards](https://oregoncheeseguild.org/rogue-river-blue-wins-worlds-best-cheese/), beating over 3,800 entries from 42 countries. It was the first American cheese to take the top prize.

The broader Rogue Valley wine region surrounds Medford. The [Rogue Valley AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_Valley_AVA) — one of Southern Oregon's premier American Viticultural Areas — benefits from warm, dry summers and cool nights at elevations between 1,200 and 2,000 feet. Tempranillo, Syrah, Viognier, and Pinot Noir all thrive here. The [Bear Creek Wine Trail](https://www.travelmedford.org/regional-attractions) and [Applegate Wine Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applegate_Valley_AVA) offer tasting routes within easy driving distance of downtown Medford, and several tasting rooms operate in the city itself.

## The Economic Engine of Southern Oregon

Medford's economy is the most diversified in the region — and it tells a story of deliberate reinvention.

The city was platted in 1883 as a railroad stop. For most of the 20th century, timber and agriculture drove the economy. When federal timber sales declined sharply in the 1990s — a consequence of spotted owl protections and shifting forest policy — Southern Oregon's economy cratered. Towns throughout the region lost their primary industry overnight.

Medford survived by pivoting.

Healthcare became the new anchor. [Asante Rogue Regional Medical Center](https://www.asante.org/) and [Providence Medford Medical Center](https://www.providence.org/) together employ over 2,000 people, and the broader healthcare sector — including clinics, specialists, and senior services — is now the city's largest employment category. As a retirement destination with a median age of 38.9, Medford has built a healthcare infrastructure that serves not just the city but the entire region.

[Lithia Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithia_Motors) is the headline employer — a Fortune 500 company with $36.2 billion in revenue headquartered on Bartlett Street. But the economic ecosystem is broader than any single company. [Harry & David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_%26_David) maintains operations in Medford. [Amy's Kitchen](https://www.amys.com/), the organic frozen food company, operates a production facility in the region. [Jackson County](https://jacksoncountyor.gov/business/economic_development/major_employers.php) lists its top employers as Asante, Lithia, Harry & David, Amy's Kitchen, Pacific Retirement Services, and Providence — a mix of healthcare, retail, food production, and senior living that no single industry shock could eliminate.

The [Medford Chamber of Commerce](https://www.medfordchamber.com/economic-and-vitality-profile/) positions the city as the commercial and professional services center for a region of over 220,000 people. If you need a specialist doctor, a corporate attorney, a commercial flight, or a Fortune 500 corporate office in Southern Oregon, you're coming to Medford.

## Britt, Jacksonville, and the Culture Next Door

Medford's most celebrated cultural asset is technically in the next town over — and that's part of the story.

[Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Oregon), five miles west of Medford, is an 1850s gold rush town with a complete National Historic Landmark downtown district. It's tiny — population around 2,800 — but it hosts the [Britt Music & Arts Festival](https://britt.org/), the Pacific Northwest's premier outdoor summer performing arts series. The festival takes place in a natural amphitheater on the hillside estate of 19th-century photographer Peter Britt, beneath ponderosa pines and native madrones, with seating for 2,200. The 2026 summer lineup includes KALEO, Pink Martini, The Marcus King Band, and a Britt Festival Orchestra run of *Star Wars: The Empire Strikes Back* performed live-to-picture.

Jacksonville exists within Medford's gravitational pull — most Britt concertgoers sleep, eat, and shop in Medford. The relationship between the two cities mirrors Ashland's with the Oregon Shakespeare Festival: a small town produces world-class art, and the larger neighboring city provides the infrastructure that makes it sustainable.

Within Medford proper, the [Holly Theatre](https://www.hollytheatre.org/) — a restored 1930 vaudeville-era movie palace — anchors downtown cultural life with a mix of films, live performances, and community events. The emerging arts district along downtown's core blocks has seen investment in recent years, adding galleries, restaurants, and small venues that give the city center more energy than its freeway-facing edges suggest.

## Housing: Affordable by Oregon Standards, Complicated by Local Ones

Medford's housing market occupies an unusual position in the Oregon landscape.

The average home value is approximately $397,600 as of early 2026, according to [Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/home-values/5891/medford-or/) — essentially flat year-over-year. The median sale price tracked by [Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/city/11999/OR/Medford/housing-market) hovers around $419,000 to $429,000, depending on the month, with homes spending an average of 24 to 38 days on market. Compared to Portland ($525,000+), Bend ($650,000+), or even Ashland ($550,000+), Medford looks like a bargain.

But the local math tells a different story. The median household income in Medford is approximately $73,230, according to [World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/us-cities/oregon/medford) — below the statewide median of roughly $85,000. The poverty rate sits at 13.17%. A $400,000 home at current mortgage rates is a significant stretch for a household earning the local median.

The Almeda Fire's destruction of affordable housing stock in neighboring Talent and Phoenix in 2020 rippled through Medford's market. Displaced families competed for limited rental and ownership inventory, tightening a market that was already constrained by geography and growth. Inventory has loosened somewhat — listings were up year-over-year through 2025 — but the gap between what the market offers and what the workforce can afford remains the central tension.

East Medford, with its views of the Table Rocks and proximity to the Rogue River corridor, commands premiums often exceeding $500,000. New construction communities like Addysen Park start in the high $370,000s. The market is fragmented — as [local analysts note](https://www.buyingsouthernoregon.com/blog/southern-oregon-housing-market-what-to-expect-in-jackson-josephine-counties), Southern Oregon in 2026 behaves as many micro-markets, not one big map.

## Getting to Medford

Medford has something almost no city its size in rural Oregon has: a real airport.

[Rogue Valley International-Medford Airport (MFR)](https://www.jacksoncountyor.gov/airport) serves direct flights to several western cities including San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver, Phoenix, Seattle, and Portland. For a city of 86,000, it's a remarkably well-connected airport — and it's the reason corporate visitors to Lithia Motors, medical patients from across the region, and tourists headed to Crater Lake or the Shakespeare Festival don't have to drive five hours from Portland.

**I-5 Exit 27** (Barnett Road) accesses south Medford and the commercial corridor. **Exit 30** (OR-62 / Crater Lake Highway) connects to east Medford and provides the primary route to Crater Lake National Park, roughly 75 miles northeast. **Exit 33** accesses north Medford and the route to Central Point and the Table Rocks.

Medford is approximately 270 miles south of Portland (4 hours), 165 miles south of Eugene (2.5 hours), and 350 miles north of San Francisco (5.5 hours). It sits at the geographic center of the Rogue Valley, with Ashland 13 miles south, Grants Pass 30 miles northwest, and Jacksonville 5 miles west.
