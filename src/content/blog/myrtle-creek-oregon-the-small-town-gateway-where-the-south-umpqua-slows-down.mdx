## The Town Named for the Creek Named for the Tree

Myrtle Creek, Oregon, is named for the creek that runs through it, which is named for the Oregon myrtle trees (also called California bay laurel) that line its banks. The town has 3,500 people, a Main Street you can walk in ten minutes, and a stretch of the South Umpqua River that — on a summer afternoon — is one of the more pleasant places to put your feet in the water between Grants Pass and Eugene.

This is not a flashy town. It doesn't have a casino, a world-famous cheese, or a gravity-defying tourist attraction. What it has is the thing that many small Oregon towns lost when the timber industry contracted: a functioning community center with parks, a river, a golf course, local businesses, and the kind of quiet competence that keeps a town of 3,500 alive in a county that's been economically challenged for decades.

The [Cow Creek Band of Umpqua Tribe of Indians](https://www.visitoregon.com/cities/myrtle-creek-oregon/) originally inhabited this area, thriving on the resources of the South Umpqua and its tributaries. European settlers arrived in the mid-1800s, drawn to the fertile land and timber. The town incorporated around the railroad and the logging industry, and while those foundations have shifted, the community has held together.

## The River and the Parks

The South Umpqua River runs along the western edge of Myrtle Creek, and river access is the town's primary outdoor draw.

[Millsite Park](https://www.cityofmyrtlecreek.com/community/rv_park/index.php), located in the heart of town adjacent to the South Umpqua, is the civic centerpiece — offering walking paths, playgrounds, picnic areas, and direct river access for swimming and fishing. The adjacent Millsite RV Park provides 11 full-hookup sites for travelers, with easy I-5 access from Exit 108.

The South Umpqua here is wide, slow, and warm in summer — ideal for wading, tubing, and casual fishing. It's not the whitewater drama of the Rogue or the canyon grandeur of the North Umpqua, but it's accessible, uncrowded, and free.

[Cougar Canyon Golf Course](https://www.tripadvisor.com/Attractions-g51984-Activities-Myrtle_Creek_Oregon.html), just two miles from Exit 108, offers a round of golf in clean air with natural wildlife and birdsong instead of freeway noise — a description that doubles as a reasonable summary of Myrtle Creek's entire appeal.

[Robert Stryk Vineyards](https://www.tripadvisor.com/Attractions-g51984-Activities-Myrtle_Creek_Oregon.html), located near town, represents the southern reach of the Umpqua Valley wine region and offers tastings in a setting that reflects the valley's boutique, family-operated winemaking culture.

## A Working-Class River Town

Myrtle Creek's economy is modest and straightforward.

The median household income is approximately $55,921, with a poverty rate of 16.19%, according to [World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/us-cities/oregon/myrtle-creek). The median age is 46.5 — older than the state average, reflecting the retirement migration that characterizes much of Douglas County. The median property value is roughly $228,200, per [Data USA](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/myrtle-creek-or) — making Myrtle Creek one of the most affordable communities on the Oregon I-5 corridor.

Most residents commute to Roseburg or other Douglas County employers. The town's commercial footprint is small but functional — a Main Street with local shops, a few restaurants, and the services that a community of 3,500 needs to operate. It's not growing fast and it's not shrinking fast. It's holding steady — which, for a small Oregon timber town in the 21st century, is its own kind of success.

## Getting to Myrtle Creek

**I-5 Exit 108** provides direct access to Myrtle Creek's Main Street and Millsite Park. **Exit 109** connects to the northern end of town.

Myrtle Creek is 21 miles south of Roseburg, 40 miles north of Grants Pass, and approximately 187 miles south of Portland on I-5.
