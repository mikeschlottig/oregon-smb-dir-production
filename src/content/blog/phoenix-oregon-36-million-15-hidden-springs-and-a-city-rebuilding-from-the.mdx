## The Man Who Stepped Into a Spring

A few weeks after the Almeda Fire burned through Phoenix, Oregon in September 2020, a hydrogeologist named Robert Coffan was walking through the scorched remains of the Bear Creek corridor near Blue Heron Park. The fire had leveled the blackberry thickets and invasive vegetation that had choked the creek banks for decades. And that's when Coffan, off-duty and not looking for anything in particular, stepped into water where there shouldn't have been any.

It was a spring. A forgotten one, buried under 75 years of overgrowth since the construction of northbound Oregon Highway 99 had redirected the waterway. Coffan's knowledge of hydrology told him to keep looking. He found another spring. Then another. Eventually, he and a growing team of volunteers and experts [mapped 15 hidden springs](https://www.oregonlive.com/environment/2025/11/oregons-miracle-mile-how-a-devastating-wildfire-led-to-environmental-restoration.html) along a stretch of Bear Creek that had been invisible to the community for generations.

The section of creek centered around Blue Heron Park became known as the **Miracle Mile**.

This is, in miniature, the story of Phoenix, Oregon. A fire exposed what was underneath. And what's being built on top of it is, in several measurable ways, better than what was there before.

## What the Fire Took

The numbers are blunt. The [Almeda Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almeda_Drive_Fire) destroyed more than 2,000 homes and 200 businesses in Phoenix on September 8, 2020. The city's fire station burned. The damage, combined with losses in neighboring Talent, displaced over 4,000 people in a single day and made the Almeda Fire [one of the most destructive in Oregon history](https://ashland.news/i-think-were-getting-there-almeda-fire-recovery-efforts-continue-in-phoenix-talent/) by structures lost.

Phoenix is small — 1.4 square miles, roughly 4,500 people at the time. Losing 2,000 structures in a city that size isn't a setback. It's an erasure of nearly half the built environment.

## $36 Million and a New City Hall

The rebuilding has been institutional, strategic, and relentless.

City Manager Eric Swanson told the [Rogue Valley Times](https://ashland.news/i-think-were-getting-there-almeda-fire-recovery-efforts-continue-in-phoenix-talent/) that Phoenix has secured **more than $36 million in federal, state, and private grants** since the fire. "Out of tragedy comes opportunity" is how Swanson summarized the experience. "Have we addressed every issue? No. This process has been, and continues to be, a marathon, not a sprint."

The most visible outcome is the **Phoenix Government and Public Safety Center** — a new 24,000-square-foot facility completed in May 2024 that combines City Hall, the Phoenix Police Department, and Jackson County Fire District 5's Station 3 under one roof. The state funded [$13.8 million](https://ktvl.com/news/local/phoenix-receives-138-million-from-state-to-rebuild-much-needed-public-service-buildings) of the project. The building replaced a fire station that burned in the Almeda Fire and a City Hall that had been outdated long before the fire arrived. The new facility was designed with [energy-efficient standards](https://blog.energytrust.org/phoenix-opens-new-energy-efficient-public-safety-building-following-2020-wildfire/) and built to serve a growing community — not just replace what was lost.

[Blue Heron Park](https://www.phoenixoregon.gov/home/public-works-engineering/infrastructure/parks/blue-heron-park/), Phoenix's signature green space, was rebuilt at a cost exceeding $500,000. FEMA covered approximately 90% of the playground reconstruction, with the remainder funded through state grants and Business Oregon. The park draws an estimated 84,000 visitors per year, according to City data reported by [Ashland News](https://ashland.news/i-think-were-getting-there-almeda-fire-recovery-efforts-continue-in-phoenix-talent/). Phoenix also won a [$50,000 T-Mobile Hometown Grant](https://travelphoenixoregon.com/stories/phoenix-rebuilds-after-tragedy-and-plans-for-future-growth/) — selected from 400 applicants nationwide — to add covered seating areas to the park.

And at the Miracle Mile, the eco-restoration project that began with Coffan's accidental discovery has reconnected the hidden springs to Bear Creek, [transforming a neglected stretch of riparian corridor](https://www.oregonlive.com/environment/2025/11/oregons-miracle-mile-how-a-devastating-wildfire-led-to-environmental-restoration.html) into a community asset. The fire, by destroying the vegetation that concealed the springs, created the conditions for something that wouldn't have happened otherwise.

## The Bear Creek Greenway and Getting Outside

Phoenix sits along the [Bear Creek Greenway](https://jacksoncountyor.gov/departments/parks/greenway/bear_creek_greenway.php), a paved multi-use trail that runs roughly 20 miles through the Rogue Valley, connecting Ashland to Central Point. The trail is one of Southern Oregon's most popular routes for cycling, running, and walking, and it passes directly through Phoenix alongside the creek and the Miracle Mile restoration area.

The surrounding Rogue Valley offers immediate access to bigger adventures. [Indigo Creek Outfitters](https://www.indigocreekoutfitters.com/), based near Phoenix, runs professionally guided whitewater rafting trips on the Rogue and Klamath Rivers — from short day floats to multi-day camping expeditions through wild and scenic canyon sections. The Cascade foothills east of town provide hiking and birdwatching, and Emigrant Lake, Ashland's popular reservoir, is a 15-minute drive south.

[Travel Southern Oregon](https://www.southernoregon.org/cities/phoenix/) positions Phoenix as a launch point for the region's outdoor recreation, with quick access to both the mountains and the river systems that define the valley.

## Small Town, Growing Fast

Phoenix's economy is modest but shifting.

The [City of Phoenix community profile](https://www.phoenixoregon.gov/community-economic-development/economic-development-2/community-profile/) describes a thriving commercial sector that supports a mix of small businesses, national chains, and specialty shops. The **Phoenix Industrial Studios** — a converted industrial space along the main corridor — has become a [local destination for shopping, dining, and wine tasting](https://travelphoenixoregon.com/things-to-do/explore-the-stores-in-the-phoenix-industrial-studios/), housing several small businesses under one roof.

The median household income is approximately $66,500, according to [Data USA](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/phoenix-or) and [city-data.com](http://city-data.com) — below the statewide median of $85,200 but a dramatic increase from $31,700 in 2000. The median age is 55.6 years, reflecting a community with a significant retiree population. Most working residents commute to Medford or other Rogue Valley employers, with an average commute of about 20 minutes.

The city is growing. Recent housing developments and annexations are projected to push the population from roughly 4,600 today to around 6,000 by 2027, according to the [City of Phoenix](https://www.phoenixoregon.gov/community-economic-development/economic-development-2/community-profile/). That growth is not accidental — it's the direct result of new housing being built in the fire footprint and beyond.

Housing affordability, as in the rest of the Rogue Valley, remains a defining challenge. The manufactured homes destroyed by the Almeda Fire were among the most affordable in the region, and their loss rippled through the valley's housing supply. Rebuilding has added new stock, but the gap between what's available and what working families can afford persists.

## Getting to Phoenix

**I-5 Exit 24** provides direct access to Phoenix, which sits along the Bear Creek Valley corridor between Talent (3 miles south) and Medford (3 miles north). The old Pacific Highway (OR-99) runs through the center of town.

[Rogue Valley International-Medford Airport (MFR)](https://www.jacksoncountyor.gov/airport) is approximately 5 miles northwest. Phoenix is roughly 295 miles south of Portland on I-5.
