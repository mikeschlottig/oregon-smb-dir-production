## The End of the Road

Portland is where I-5 ends in Oregon — or begins, depending on your direction.

Every town we've covered in this series, from Ashland at the California border to Tigard in the southwestern suburbs, feeds into this city. The agricultural production of the Willamette Valley, the timber of the southern Oregon mountains, the commuters of Salem and Woodburn and Wilsonville — they all converge here, at the confluence of the Willamette and Columbia rivers, where 627,000 people live in the 28th most populous city in the United States.

The Marquam Bridge — the double-decked I-5 crossing of the Willamette, northbound on top, southbound below — carries over 140,000 vehicles per day, making it the busiest crossing in Oregon. It's the bottleneck, the gateway, and the symbol of a city that has always been defined by its relationship to water and the roads that connect it to everywhere else.

## The Food City

Portland's food scene is not a scene. It's an economy.

The city is consistently ranked among the top food cities in America, and the reason is structural: Portland sits at the center of some of the richest agricultural land in the Pacific Northwest. The Willamette Valley provides grass-fed beef, hazelnuts, berries, and produce. The Oregon coast delivers seafood. The Columbia Gorge grows fruit. And the city itself has cultivated a food culture that values creativity, process, and an almost militant commitment to local sourcing.

The food cart pods — clusters of mobile food vendors serving everything from Thai to Ethiopian to Korean-Mexican fusion — are Portland's most visible culinary innovation. But the deeper story is in the restaurants: chef-driven establishments that would command Michelin attention in any other city, operating in a market where diners expect excellence and affordability in equal measure.

The craft beer industry was essentially invented here (or at least perfected). The coffee roasting scene rivals Seattle's. The distillery culture is growing. And the farm-to-table movement, which in most cities is a marketing concept, is in Portland a supply chain reality — the farms are 30 minutes away.

## Two Rivers, Twelve Bridges, One City

Portland straddles the Willamette River, with the west side (downtown, the Pearl District, Nob Hill, the West Hills) and the east side (the neighborhoods that contain most of Portland's character: Hawthorne, Division, Alberta, Mississippi, St. Johns) connected by twelve bridges. The Columbia River forms the northern border with Washington state, and the I-5 bridge across it is one of the most congested crossings on the West Coast.

Forest Park, at 5,200 acres, is one of the largest urban forests in the United States. The International Rose Test Garden in Washington Park has been growing roses since 1917 — the reason Portland is called the Rose City. The Japanese Garden, adjacent to the rose garden, is considered one of the most authentic Japanese gardens outside Japan.

## The Numbers and the Narrative

Portland's population is approximately 627,000, per World Population Review, **declining at -0.69% annually** — a loss of roughly 26,000 people since the 2020 census. The metro area, at 2.29 million, continues to grow (0.97% annually), meaning people are leaving the city for the suburbs, not leaving the region.

The **median household income is $90,919** with a poverty rate of 12.75%. The median age is 38.8. The city is the county seat of Multnomah County, Oregon's most populous county, and extends into both Washington and Clackamas counties.

Portland's population decline has dominated headlines, but the metro area's continued growth suggests a regional story that's more nuanced than the city-limits data implies. Washington County added the most new residents of any Oregon county in 2025. Clackamas County grew modestly. The metro is evolving, not shrinking — dispersing into the suburban ring that includes every city we've profiled from Wilsonville north.

## The Culture

Powell's City of Books — a full city block of new and used books in the Pearl District — is the largest independent bookstore in the world. The Portland Saturday Market, operating since 1974, is the nation's largest continuously operating open-air arts and crafts market. The Portland Trail Blazers play at the Moda Center. The Portland Art Museum is the oldest art museum on the West Coast.

The city's cultural identity is famously difficult to pin down. "Keep Portland Weird" is the bumper sticker, but the weirdness has always been more about creative independence than performative oddity. Portland is a city of makers, DIY entrepreneurs, small-batch producers, and people who moved here because they wanted to live differently — and then built the infrastructure to make that possible.

## Getting to Portland

**I-5 Exits 292 through 308** serve the Portland metro. Exit 299A (OR-43/Macadam Avenue) provides access to the inner southwest. Exit 300 connects to I-84 East toward the Columbia Gorge and Portland International Airport (PDX). I-405 loops through downtown.

Portland is 47 miles north of Salem, 110 miles north of Eugene, 175 miles north of Roseburg, and 285 miles north of Ashland — the full length of the I-5 Oregon corridor.

Portland International Airport (PDX), consistently rated one of the best airports in the U.S., provides domestic and international service.
