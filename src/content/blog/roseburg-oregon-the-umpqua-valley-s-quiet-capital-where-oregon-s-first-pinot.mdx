## Oregon's First Pinot Noir

In 1961, a man named Richard Sommer drove from the University of California, Davis — where he'd studied viticulture — to a piece of hillside property near Roseburg, Oregon, and planted grapevines. Among them: Pinot Noir.

This was, by every expert assessment available at the time, a terrible idea. California's wine establishment had declared Oregon too cold and too wet to produce quality wine grapes. No commercial vineyard existed in the state. Sommer planted anyway.

[HillCrest Vineyard](https://www.oregonwine.org/regions/umpqua-valley/) became Oregon's first post-Prohibition commercial winery. The Pinot Noir Sommer planted near Roseburg predates the Willamette Valley plantings that would later make Oregon world-famous for the varietal. The Umpqua Valley — not the Willamette — is where Oregon wine began.

Today, the [Umpqua Valley AVA](https://www.umpquavalleywineries.org/wine-facts/) encompasses over 30 wineries and 70 vineyards with more than 4,000 planted acres. The region's climate is one of Oregon's most diverse — cooler northern reaches support Pinot Noir and Riesling, while the warmer southern portions grow Tempranillo, Syrah, and Viognier. Over 40 grape varieties thrive here. The majority of wineries are small, family-owned operations where you're likely to meet the winemaker during your tasting.

Roseburg sits at the center of this wine geography — and the fact that most people associate Oregon wine exclusively with the Willamette Valley is precisely the kind of assumption that makes the Umpqua Valley interesting.

## The Road to Crater Lake

Roseburg is one of two primary gateways to Oregon's only national park.

[Oregon Route 138](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Route_138) — the North Umpqua Highway — departs I-5 at Exit 124 and winds east along the North Umpqua River for roughly 80 miles to Crater Lake National Park. The drive is, by most accounts, one of the most scenic highway experiences in Oregon: waterfalls, old-growth forest, fly-fishing access, and the turquoise-green river visible from the road for much of the route.

The North Umpqua is itself a destination. It's one of Oregon's premier fly-fishing rivers — summer steelhead are the main draw, running from June through October in water so clear you can watch the fish from the bank. The [Umpqua Hot Springs](https://traveloregon.com/places-to-go/cities/roseburg/), Susan Creek Falls, Toketee Falls, and Watson Falls are all accessible from OR-138 between Roseburg and the park.

The southern route to Crater Lake — via OR-62 from Medford — is more commonly traveled. But the northern route from Roseburg offers a quieter, more immersive approach that rewards the extra time.

## The Timber Town That Pivoted

Roseburg's economic identity has shifted three times in its 150-year history.

It was a railroad town first — the Douglas County seat, established in the 1850s, grew around the Oregon and California Railroad corridor. Timber became the dominant industry in the early 20th century, and for decades Roseburg was a mill town. When the federal timber sales collapsed in the 1990s, the economic shock hit Douglas County harder than almost anywhere in Oregon. Mills closed. Population growth stalled. Poverty rates rose.

The pivot came through diversification, not replacement. Healthcare emerged as the largest employment sector. [Mercy Medical Center](https://www.chifranciscan.org/mercy-medical-center-roseburg.html) and the VA Roseburg Healthcare System are major employers. Roseburg Forest Products — a plywood and engineered wood products company — maintains headquarters here, keeping the timber connection alive in a different form. Retail, education (Umpqua Community College), and county government round out the employment base.

The economy employs about 10,000 people in the city proper. The median household income is approximately $52,928 — $53,918 depending on source, per [World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/us-cities/oregon/roseburg) and Oregon demographics data. The poverty rate is 22.4% — one of the highest among Oregon's mid-size cities, reflecting the long-term structural challenges of a timber-dependent economy in transition.

## Housing: Genuinely Affordable

Roseburg is one of the few mid-size Oregon cities where housing remains within reach of median-income households.

Home values are significantly lower than the Rogue Valley to the south or the Willamette Valley to the north. The median property value is roughly $250,000–$280,000, depending on the source and neighborhood — a price point that a household earning the local median income can realistically afford at current mortgage rates.

The tradeoff is opportunity. Roseburg's job market is smaller and lower-paying than Eugene, Salem, or Medford. For remote workers, retirees, or people willing to commute, the combination of affordable housing, wine country access, river recreation, and a functioning county seat with full services makes Roseburg genuinely compelling. For people dependent on local employment, the math is tighter.

## Getting to Roseburg

**I-5 Exit 124** (OR-138 / Diamond Lake Boulevard) is the primary access to central Roseburg and the route east to Crater Lake. **Exit 119** (Garden Valley Boulevard) serves south Roseburg. **Exit 125** accesses north Roseburg and the route to Sutherlin.

Roseburg is approximately 70 miles south of Eugene (1 hour), 110 miles north of Medford (1.5 hours), and 180 miles south of Portland (3 hours) on I-5. The closest commercial airport is Eugene Airport (EUG), 75 miles north.
