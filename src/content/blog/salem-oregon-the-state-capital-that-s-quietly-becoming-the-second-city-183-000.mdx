## Oregon's Capital, Oregon's Second City

Salem has spent most of its modern history being the city that isn't Portland.

This is unfair but understandable. Portland has the brand — the food scene, the weirdness, the international airport, the cultural cachet. Salem has the state government, the prisons, the agricultural economy, and the reputation of a mid-sized city that people drive through on I-5 without stopping.

That's changing, slowly and deliberately.

Salem's population is approximately 182,600, per World Population Review, growing at 0.61% annually — steady, consistent growth that has added nearly 7,000 people since the 2020 census. The city is the second-largest in Oregon, the capital of the state, the county seat of Marion County, and the center of the Mid-Willamette Valley — Oregon's most productive agricultural region.

The city was founded in 1842, became the capital of the Oregon Territory in 1851, and was incorporated in 1857. It sits alongside the Willamette River, which forms the boundary between Marion and Polk counties. The West Salem neighborhood, across the river, is technically in Polk County — giving Salem the unusual distinction of straddling two counties.

## The Government City

State government is Salem's economic anchor. The Oregon State Capitol, the state courts, and dozens of state agencies employ thousands of workers. Government employment provides stability — Salem doesn't experience the boom-and-bust cycles of tech-dependent or single-industry cities — but it also creates a ceiling. Government salaries are steady but rarely spectacular, and the culture of a capital city can feel bureaucratic to outsiders.

The median household income is $75,487, per World Population Review, with a poverty rate of 14.37%. The median age is 36. These numbers reflect a diverse city with a significant Hispanic/Latino population (approximately 25%), a large state-employee workforce, and the economic stratification that comes with being a regional center for both professional services and agricultural labor.

## Willamette University and the Downtown

Willamette University, founded in 1842, is the oldest university in the western United States. The campus sits adjacent to the Oregon State Capitol in downtown Salem, creating an unusual pairing of academic and governmental architecture. The Hallie Ford Museum of Art, on the Willamette campus, houses one of the more significant art collections in the state.

Salem's historic downtown has been through cycles of decline and revitalization. The current trajectory is upward — new restaurants, a growing craft beverage scene, revitalized commercial spaces, and the kind of incremental downtown investment that signals confidence without hype. The Riverfront Park and carousel along the Willamette provide public green space, and Bush's Pasture Park — a 90-acre urban park — offers gardens, trails, and the historic Bush House Museum.

## The Agricultural Capital

Salem sits in the center of the Mid-Willamette Valley, which is the top producer of agricultural products in Oregon. The valley produces nursery stock, grass seed, berries, wine grapes, hops, hazelnuts, and vegetables. Salem's "Cherry City" nickname dates from the era when the region's cherry orchards dominated the landscape — and while cherry production has declined, the agricultural economy remains foundational.

The city has business-friendly taxes and fees, per the City of Salem's economic data, which helps attract companies beyond the government sector. Salem is strong in traded industry sectors, meaning it produces goods sold outside the area — a sign of economic health that goes beyond local consumption.

## The Numbers

Salem's population of 182,600 makes it the second-largest city in Oregon. The city grew by nearly 4% since 2020 and is projected to continue growing as Portland's housing costs push more residents south along the I-5 corridor. Salem is 47 miles from Portland — close enough for a commute (albeit a long one), far enough for its own identity.

The city's diversity is notable: approximately 25% Hispanic/Latino, significantly more diverse than most Oregon cities outside Portland. This diversity shapes the food scene, the cultural calendar, and the community's character in ways that many visitors don't expect from a state capital in the Pacific Northwest.

## Getting to Salem

**I-5 Exits 248 through 260** provide access to Salem and its surrounding communities. Exit 253 (Highway 22/Mission Street) is the primary access to downtown Salem and Willamette University.

Salem is 47 miles south of Portland, 25 miles north of Albany, 65 miles north of Eugene, and centrally located in the Willamette Valley. The Salem Municipal Airport (SLE) serves general aviation; Portland International Airport (PDX) is the nearest commercial airport, approximately one hour north.
