## The Town Behind the Fairy Tale

Every Oregonian of a certain age has a memory of Enchanted Forest.

Since 1971, this handmade amusement park off I-5 at Exit 248 has been a rite of passage for families across the state. Roger Tofte, a retired construction worker, spent years building the park by hand — carving storybook characters, constructing rides, and creating a woodland fantasy that somehow manages to be both charmingly amateur and genuinely magical. The park has water rides, a haunted house, a bobsled roller coaster, and enough fairy-tale whimsy to fill a childhood's worth of memories.

Enchanted Forest is not a corporate theme park. It's a family operation that has survived for over half a century on charm, nostalgia, and the willingness of Oregon parents to drive their kids to Exit 248 every summer. For many Oregonians, it's the first theme park they ever visited — and the one they remember most fondly.

Turner is the town that holds this particular piece of Oregon identity. And while Enchanted Forest is the draw, Turner itself is worth understanding.

## The Turner Family Legacy

The town's name comes from Henry Turner, a 19th-century landowner who shaped the area's early development. Turner and his sons built a flour mill on their farm in 1863 — a significant investment that anchored the surrounding community. Turner also helped plat the neighboring town of Aumsville, named for his son-in-law Amos "Aumus" Davis, who died before the mill was completed.

The connection between Turner and Aumsville runs deep — two towns, one family, and a shared history of settlement, agriculture, and the kind of pragmatic community-building that defined the mid-Willamette Valley in the 1860s. Turner was incorporated by the Oregon Legislative Assembly on February 10, 1905.

## Quiet Prosperity

Turner's numbers tell a story of comfortable stability.

The population is approximately 2,396, per World Population Review, declining slightly at -0.37% annually. But the economic picture is strong: the **median household income is $101,979** with a **poverty rate of just 3.88%** — one of the lowest of any town on the I-5 corridor.

The median age is 45.4, older than most corridor towns, reflecting established homeowners and empty-nesters rather than young families. Turner is the kind of place where people arrive, buy a home, and stay — a quiet Marion County community with Salem's services 10 miles north and the rural countryside at the back door.

## Getting to Turner

**I-5 Exit 248** (Sunnyside/Turner) provides access to Turner. The town is approximately 2 miles east of the interchange.

Turner is 10 miles south of Salem, 25 miles north of Albany, and approximately 55 miles south of Portland on I-5.
